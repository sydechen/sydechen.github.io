Case Study — AI / Healthcare UX

Predictive Analytics

2022 – 2025
AI / ML Healthcare Data Visualisation User Testing Figma
01 — Starting Point

Inheriting a project mid-stream

In 2021, a team began developing an ML model to predict a patient's risk of developing chronic kidney disease (CKD). When I took over design in 2022, one user study had already been completed.

My task: design a pop-out that conveys the model's risk prediction to a primary care doctor in seconds — clearly and credibly.

Design concepts explored in the 2021 user study

02 — Discovery

Getting familiar with the model

I reviewed the 2021 study findings. Four key takeaways shaped my approach:

→

Doctors thought the tool would be useful and would likely prompt earlier referrals to nephrology.

→

Doctors would trust the tool if they could see what's underlying the predictions, or if it had been validated in a study.

→

Many thought it would be useful for both doctor and patient to see which health factors contributed to the model's prediction.

→

Simple visualisations work best for a tool like this.

I also spent several days talking with the PM, tech lead, and a doctor on my team — learning that the model would predict risk of stage 1–2 CKD progressing to stage 3 within up to 5 years, and that contributing factors per patient could be visualised.

Visualisations of contributing factors for two test patients, created by the modelling team

03 — Research

Mockups & user testing

Our teams wanted to display 1-, 2-, and 3-year risk scores. I created several design options for the visualisation, colours, and labelling, then partnered with a UX researcher to test them with primary care doctors.

1

Colour mapping was intuitive

Most expected green, yellow, and red to indicate low, moderate, and high risk.

2

2-year scores weren't useful

1-year, 3-year, and 5-year time frames were all perceived as valuable — 2-year was dropped.

3

Show values, don't hide them

Most liked percentages and values on the contributing factors visualisation. Most also wanted the visualisation visible without expanding a fold.

4

Clinical nuance matters

Some wanted stage 3 CKD to be further split into 3A and 3B.

04 — Resulting Design

The winning design

The tree-style visualisation won in testing. Based on feedback, I added percentages to the contributing factors and confirmed with the modelling team whether the prediction covered stage 3A or 3B. I updated time frames from 1-, 2-, 3-year to 1-, 3-, and 5-year.

User study findings table, colour-coded for pattern analysis

05 — An Unexpected Turn

Legal implications

Some tools require rigorous testing cycles before they can display a number about a patient. Our legal team determined this was one of those tools — a process that could take years.

In the meantime, my team opted to water down the language. Instead of numbers, the output became "patient requires a closer review or intervention" or "patient does not require a closer review or intervention". We kept the red/green shading to visually differentiate outcomes.

I also created a text-only version of the contributing factors — simpler to build and avoiding any number-based display.

06 — Reflection

In conclusion

Working with predictive analytics has been exciting. The core challenge was turning raw model output into something a doctor could trust and understand in a few seconds.

Next time I get a chance to do something like this, I'll talk to the lawyers a little earlier in the process.

This project was quickly followed by two more risk models by the same team — one for Diabetic Foot Ulcer and one for Hypertensive Crisis Onset. Both were built faster because we reused the design framework established here.
