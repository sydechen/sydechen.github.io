# Sydney — UX Identity Brief

## Positioning

Sydney solves problems from the concept phase forward, thinking strategically across business, engineering, and user needs, and turns that thinking into visually polished, easy-to-understand results. Her core skill is taking complicated concepts and simplifying or visualizing them so people understand faster and more clearly.

## Point of view

Sydney takes complicated concepts and simplifies or visualizes them so people can understand them more quickly and clearly. She believes in democratizing processes and information — access shouldn't be gatekept to experts. She pushes back on obfuscation of background processes and reasoning: if someone wants to do or know something, there should be a clear way for them to get there.

Her underlying goal is to improve health outcomes for patients and make healthcare more accessible. Fixing obfuscated logic and opaque pricing is one concrete lever toward that — if patients can compare prices, know costs upfront, and understand coverage at the point of care, they're more likely to actually get the care they need.

## What makes her background distinct

Sydney's UX work is grounded in healthcare, specifically time spent interviewing, shadowing, and studying clinicians. Her work has spanned point-of-care clinical guidance for primary care providers (guideline-based recommendations for chronic disease areas like type 2 diabetes, HFrEF, and depression), patient history timelines, patient risk predictions, LLM-based patient context tools, population health solutions like worklists and dashboards, and CMS regulatory solutions such as documentation templates and rules. One body of that work studied content authors who write executable clinical decision support (CDS) — the clinical rules, measures, and definitions that run on clinician computers, standardize care, and help clinicians share information across systems.

She's also shadowed clinicians directly. In one project on knee and hip replacement outcomes, shadowing an orthopedic specialist overturned an assumption baked into the whole research direction: that physicians were the primary decision-makers on surgery. In the room, patients directed their own care — active, opinionated participants, not passive recipients of doctor's orders. That shifted her team's whole approach: influencing the physician was only part of solving the problem; reaching the patient directly mattered just as much, if not more.

That instinct — to go verify the assumption in person rather than trust the org chart or the stated process — is part of what she brings to any UX problem, healthcare or not.

Healthcare is her only industry experience to date, so it should stay one thread in her body of work rather than the headline — the instinct to verify assumptions in person, not the industry itself, is what would carry into any other domain.

## Voice and tone

Analytical and conversational in roughly equal parts. In practice that means:

- Explains concepts by building them up piece by piece (define the term, give a concrete example, then extend the example) rather than starting from an abstract definition.
- States opinions plainly and personally ("my own goals are clear," "I understand a good amount of this is due to...") rather than hedging behind passive voice.
- Uses short, declarative sentences to state a stance, then unpacks the reasoning after — the stance comes first, not last.
- Comfortable naming a wrong assumption she made ("I learned that I had made a big assumption") as part of telling the story, not as a confession to soften.
- Grounds abstract points in one specific, concrete example rather than several generic ones (one measure — "High Blood Pressure" — not a list of examples).
- Not clinical or corporate — avoid jargon-as-default, buzzwords ("synergy," "leverage," "holistic"), and hedge-everything qualifier stacking.

When writing portfolio copy in her voice: write plainly, take a real stance instead of a neutral survey of options, and use one well-chosen concrete example over three generic ones.

## Raw voice samples

Her own paragraphs, unedited, for direct reference on sentence rhythm and word choice.

**Explaining a complex concept:**

> A content author covers a wide sweep of different people who create things from educational material to opinion pieces. What I studied were content authors who create executable clinical decision support (CDS). The CDS part of that encompasses clinical rules, measures, and definitions. "Executable" means that they can be run on a computer. So one common activity of these content authors is writing a clinical measure that runs on clinician computers to tell them how their patients stand against a measure. For example, a measure could be "High Blood Pressure". If patients have blood pressure greater than 140/90, then the measure would say they have high blood pressure. Otherwise, they would not. The logic behind this measure needs to be written by a content author. The logic can be extended upon to form rules, like "If this patient has high blood pressure and is not on a blood pressure medication, then recommend putting them on medication". This kind of content created by these authors helps clinicians better understand their patients, more easily share that information with other clinicians, and set care standards. In the background, logic like this makes it easier for rules and definitions to be standardized across systems.

**Taking a stance:**

> Something I dislike in healthcare tech is the obfuscation of logic and rules and lack of transparency in pricing. I understand a good amount of this is due to the competitive nature of the industry, but it gets in the way of better patient care and creates frustration for everyone. For example, why can't a patient or their provider see easily whether or not a certain treatment option will be covered, and how much it will cost the patient? Insurance companies have to make coverage and appropriateness decisions, so why can't they tell patients at the time of considering that treatment option what that insurance decision will be? Our goals in healthcare tech are always clashing, but my own goals are clear. Let patients compare prices across practices. Tell patients how much something will cost and then bill them that amount. Make coverage easy to understand when providers and patients are looking at treatment options. Make healthcare a better user experience, and more people will use it.

**Telling a research story:**

> About a year ago, I went with my team to visit an orthopedic practice where we shadowed orthopedic specialists and talked to their care teams. We had been exploring a problem in this space for the past year — that there was a significant portion of knee and hip replacement patients that did not see meaningful improvement in function after their recovery period. Based on the subject matter experts on my team, my hypothesis was that if we identified the "problem" patients who would be unlikely to see improvement after surgery, then orthopedic specialists would avoid suggesting surgery to those patients and suggest alternative options. At the orthopedic practice, I learned that I had made a big assumption. I had assumed that the orthopedic specialists were making the decisions, "yes, you need surgery" or "no, you don't need surgery", and that the patients would take their advice as gospel. While shadowing, I saw a different dynamic. The orthopedist I was shadowing was a super friendly guy. He was warm and talkative with his patients, and his patients were warm and talkative back. His patients told him their stories. He listened and sympathized. Some encounters were jovial and full of back and forth jokes, while others were tearful. When they talked about treatment options, the patients did not sit back and nod quietly. They were active and opinionated participants, and the orthopedist was more likely to be the one nodding. The patients directed their care. If they wanted surgery, then as long as there were no physical barriers, then surgery was scheduled. After this visit, my thinking shifted. If we want to reduce unhelpful surgeries, influencing the physician is only part of the picture. The other half, or perhaps the other larger portion, is reaching the patient.

**Reflecting on why she does this work:**

> Well, I fell into UX in a way. I didn't really know it was a thing until junior year of college getting my bachelors degree in Comp Sci. By then, I was starting to realize that coding software was not my passion. A friend suggested I take a course that focused mainly on web design, outside of the comp sci courses and in the media journalism school. That was where I learned about UX. I became interested in interface design in that class. When I got a job training opportunity after graduating, I chose to focus on training under a UX leader. It was fun! I got to problem solve and define solutions in a visual way. I love visual arts and using imagery to communicate. I like thinking about how to show people things in a way that will make sense and be useful to them. In the last few years it's become more about solving problems at a deeper level. That involves talking to people and trying to understand deeply what they do. I think this gives me a sense of meaning, trying to learn about people and figure out how to help them.
